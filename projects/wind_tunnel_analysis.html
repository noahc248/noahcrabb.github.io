Boundary Layer Wind Tunnel Analysis

A report-driven experimental study of turbulent boundary-layer growth in the Virginia Tech Stability Wind Tunnel, using a 30-port pneumatic rake to compare two sting geometries, track wall-profile growth between stations, and evaluate where real wind-tunnel flow departs from ideal flat-plate scaling.

Overview

What was being measured and why it mattered

This experiment focused on how a support sting alters the tunnel-wall boundary layer before a full model is ever mounted to it. That matters because support hardware can introduce blockage, adverse pressure gradients, and horseshoe-vortex structures that contaminate the local flow seen by the actual test article.

The work therefore served two purposes at once: first, it produced a direct experimental comparison between no-fillet and filleted sting geometries, and second, it created validation-oriented insight for future CFD work by showing where simple turbulent flat-plate expectations stop matching the real tunnel environment.

Facility and environment

The testing was performed in the Virginia Tech Stability Wind Tunnel, a closed-loop subsonic facility with a 1.85 m by 1.85 m hard-wall test section.

Measurement hardware

The 154.2 mm rake used 30 pressure ports, with fine spacing near the wall and logarithmically wider spacing farther out to capture the full profile.

Data goal

The end product was not just a plot set; it was a quantified estimate of boundary-layer thickness, growth rate, geometry effect, and agreement with turbulent power-law scaling.

Experimental Method

Instrumentation, acquisition, and data reduction

The pressure rake was mounted perpendicular to the wall and connected to a DTC Initium ESP32HD scanner with a reported accuracy of plus or minus 0.05% over a plus or minus 20 inWC range. At each Reynolds-number condition, 25 pressure records were collected and then processed in MATLAB.

  • Total-pressure measurements from the rake and local static-pressure measurements from the panel were converted into local velocities using Bernoulli-based reduction for steady incompressible flow.
  • Profiles were normalized as u/Uinf versus wall-normal position y so the shape and thickness of the turbulent boundary layer could be compared cleanly across Reynolds-number conditions.
  • Boundary-layer thickness was defined by interpolation to the 99% free-stream location rather than direct pointwise measurement, which is why probe spacing and local free-stream definition mattered so much to accuracy.
  • The report also includes an uncertainty appendix that propagates uncertainty through local velocity, normalized velocity, and inferred thickness calculations using root-sum-square methods and perturbation-based sensitivity.
Results

What the data showed about the sting and the boundary layer

The measured profiles showed the expected turbulent shape: low velocity at the wall, rapid near-wall growth, and eventual approach to the local free stream. Across most of the sweep, higher Reynolds-number conditions produced thinner boundary layers, although the highest condition showed a reversal of that trend, which the report correctly treats as a sign of experimental inconsistency or additional flow disturbance.

Geometry effect

The fillet did not uniformly reduce thickness at every condition, but it did reduce the average streamwise boundary-layer growth rate between stations from about 0.01236 to 0.01093. That is a meaningful result because it suggests the fillet softened the sting-induced disturbance even when thickness differences at a single station were small.

Theory comparison

After calibrating a turbulent power-law model at one station, the predicted thicknesses at the second station were still systematically larger than the measured values. That mismatch shows the flow was not behaving like an ideal smooth flat plate and was instead being altered by sting blockage, pressure-gradient effects, and likely vortex-driven distortion.

One of the strongest parts of this project is that it does not force the data to agree with simple theory. The report uses the discrepancy itself as the engineering result, which is exactly the right interpretation for a disturbed experimental flowfield.

Technical Takeaways

Why this is more than a lab write-up

This project demonstrates experimental aerodynamics in the full engineering sense: understanding the hardware, collecting a repeatable dataset, choosing a reduction method, evaluating where a simple model fails, and then turning that mismatch into something useful for later testing and CFD validation.

It involved instrumentation decisions, velocity reconstruction from pressure, profile normalization, interpolation for 99% thickness, geometry-to-geometry comparison, and a theory-versus-measurement assessment rooted in the actual physics of tunnel interference.
