Overview

Competition aircraft built around lightweight printability and stable flight

"Hokie Tail" is a fully 3D-printed competition aircraft developed by Virginia Tech's 3DPAC team. The design problem was not just to make a printable airplane, but to make one that could stay aloft, survive repeated iteration, and keep its geometry aligned well enough to fly efficiently after assembly. That forced every major decision to sit at the intersection of aerodynamics, structure, printer capability, and field-service practicality.

My work on the aircraft centered on detailed CAD development and design integration across the wing, fuselage, boom, and tail. The final configuration used a vase-mode main wing for aggressive weight reduction, a lofted fuselage with embedded electronics access and wing support features, TPU-based hinge and latch solutions, and a redesigned NylonX boom after flight testing exposed stability limits in the first tail configuration.

Wing strategy

The wing geometry was designed specifically around spiral-vase manufacturing, which meant the internal structure had to remain compatible with one continuous outer-wall toolpath.

Fuselage strategy

The fuselage packaged propulsion, wiring, access, and wing attachment into smooth lofted geometry while preserving tight interfaces and low mass.

Tail strategy

Flight testing drove a boom redesign toward a larger hybrid section and NylonX construction to suppress wobble and recover control effectiveness.

Wing Design

Designing a wing that could actually be printed in spiral vase mode

The most distinctive structural decision in the aircraft was the use of spiral vase printing for the main wing and winglets. That choice saves significant mass, but it also creates a hard CAD constraint: internal structure cannot exist as disconnected geometry. If a feature does not tie into the continuous exterior surface, it will not be produced by the printer's single-wall spiral path.

To solve that, the wing's internal structure was modeled as thin 0.1 mm bodies representing a diamond reinforcement pattern and spar channel, then split and subtracted from the solid wing body. This creates negative space while preserving a printable continuous shell. Even the intentional gaps needed to maintain the toolpath were kept small enough that the print would fuse into a lightweight but still coherent structure during manufacture.

  • The full wing was partitioned into nine printable pieces: five main wing sections, two winglets, and two aileron elements.
  • The five main wing sections and the winglets were intended for vase-mode printing, while the ailerons used a separate hollow 0.8 mm skin-thickness construction.
  • Ailerons were connected with TPU hinges so the control-surface joint remained lightweight, compliant, and directly compatible with printed assembly channels.
  • A servo recess was cut directly into one main wing section, accepting a localized overhang in exchange for tighter integration and cleaner packaging.

The key engineering value of the wing is not just that it is light. The geometry was deliberately authored so the manufacturing method and structural concept are the same idea, rather than two separate decisions fighting each other later in the build.

Fuselage Integration

Lofted fuselage geometry built around propulsion, access, and load transfer

The fuselage was created using loft-based geometry driven by carefully placed sketches and offset planes. That approach gave precise control over diameter transitions, internal cut features, and alignment interfaces without sacrificing smooth outer surfaces. Instead of treating the fuselage as a simple shell, the design assigned each segment a specific job in propulsion support, electronics packaging, wing load transfer, or tail attachment.

  • The nose cone included a 1 inch tip fillet, four 5 mm motor-mount holes, a 10 mm central shaft opening, and a 13 mm solid depth to resist thrust loads and screw pull-through.
  • The electronics bay maintained a constant 32 mm diameter over a 215 mm length with 0.8 mm walls, plus a top escape hatch retained by printed TPU bands instead of heavier secondary hardware.
  • The wing-cutout section held the same 32 mm diameter over 173 mm and was modeled with essentially no clearance so the wing-fuselage interface stayed tight and shear loads could be shared through the wing bed.
  • The aft cone lofted from 32 mm down to 12.77 mm over 40 mm, then transitioned into a keyed boom interface to prevent rotation and preserve tail alignment.

Wiring paths were integrated through the nose, electronics bay, wing section, and aft fuselage so the receiver, ESC, servos, and battery could be packaged internally without cluttering the external flow or creating fragile ad hoc routing during final assembly.

Tail Architecture

Boom and stabilizer design driven by stiffness, print quality, and controllability

The boom was one of the most important structural members in the aircraft because it had to carry a long moment arm without allowing the tail to wobble under aerodynamic load. The final boom used a 12.77 mm diameter circular tube with 2 mm walls and an internal 10 mm by 4 mm rectangular hollow box section, combining torsional stiffness from the outer circular shell with extra resistance to horizontal bending from the internal core.

Tail surfaces

The horizontal and vertical stabilizers were ultimately modeled as flat 3 mm thick plates because a more traditional thin tail airfoil printed too weakly and warped near the trailing edges. A sweep along guide rails replaced a loft-based workflow, which made iteration faster and preserved a constant printable thickness across the parts.

Control integration

Rudder and elevator motion used TPU hinges and rod-driven servos. The elevator included a central gap for boom and vertical-tail clearance, and material was removed so one servo could actuate the elevator cleanly while keeping both sides synchronized.

A dedicated servo mount was also designed to slide onto the boom, using two circular boom pass-throughs, 0.8 mm walls, and a thicker 3 mm base plate with 2.1 mm screw holes. That made the tail controls a deliberate subsystem instead of an afterthought attached late in assembly.

Manufacturing

Material and printer selection were part of the design, not a downstream choice

The aircraft was built around a mixed-material additive workflow using four Bambu Lab P1S printers, two Creality K2 Plus printers, and one Creality CR-30. Different structures were assigned to different machines and materials based on what each part needed structurally and what the printer could reliably produce.

LW-PLA airframe

Wing, fuselage, and tail components were printed in ColorFabb lightweight PLA, with the wing taking advantage of spiral vase mode and a reported average mass reduction of about 30 percent relative to more traditional LW-PLA prints.

NylonX boom

The boom was printed in carbon-fiber-infused PA-12 NylonX on the K2 Plus because the heated chamber and hardened components were needed for that material and for the final stiffness target.

TPU functional parts

TPU was used for both hinge lines and fuselage retention bands, giving the design compliant joints and closures without adding metal hardware or separate latch mechanisms.

The spar strategy also matched the manufacturing plan. A solid PLA spar was printed as one long piece on the CR-30's infinite-Z platform, then threaded through the wing during assembly alongside servo wiring. Because the wing's internal vase-mode structure is directly connected to the outer skin, assembly required careful routing and alignment to avoid damaging the lightweight internal geometry.

Control-surface refinement continued at the manufacturing level as well. Reducing TPU hinge thickness from 0.8 mm to 0.3 mm improved deflection range, and taller control horns were used where longer pusher-rod travel was needed before interference with the surrounding structure.

Iteration

Flight testing exposed instability, and the redesign addressed the real failure mode

One of the clearest examples of engineering iteration in the project came from the tail boom. Initial flight tests with a standard PLA boom showed significant wobble, which reduced aircraft stability and degraded rudder and elevator effectiveness. That is exactly the kind of issue that looks acceptable in a CAD assembly but becomes obvious once the airframe is carrying aerodynamic load.

  • The boom diameter was increased from 8 mm to 12.77 mm to improve global stiffness.
  • A rectangular internal section was added to resist horizontal bending more effectively than a simple thin tube.
  • The servo mount location was shifted toward the nose to reduce the moment introduced by the tail-control hardware.
  • The boom material was upgraded from standard PLA to NylonX to gain rigidity and strength while controlling mass.

The report identifies two central innovations in the final aircraft: the vase-mode-compatible wing internal structure and the use of a boom in place of a conventional rear fuselage volume. Together, those choices removed mass where the aircraft could least afford it, but they only worked because the team was willing to revise the structural details after seeing how the first configuration behaved in real flight testing.

One of the strongest future-looking ideas in the report is the proposed NylonX spar. The team estimated that a smoother, better-fitting carbon-fiber-nylon spar could reduce both wing weight and flutter further, but held off until print quality was good enough for reliable insertion through the wing channel.
